Congress has not declared war since 1942. Subsequent military actions have proceeded under Authorizations for Use of Military Force (AUMFs), several of which have been used by multiple administrations far beyond their original scope.

In December 2025, Congress repealed the 1991 Gulf War authorization and the 2002 Iraq AUMF through the FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act — the first time it has clawed back a war authorization since 1971. That leaves the 2001 AUMF, passed three days after 9/11, which has been used by four administrations to justify military operations in over a dozen countries — including operations against organizations that did not exist when the AUMF was passed.

Repeal of these stale authorizations produced bipartisan majorities in both chambers over several sessions. Senators Tim Kaine and Todd Young led the effort across multiple Congresses, and the December 2025 repeal of the 1991 and 2002 authorizations showed the coalition can deliver.

The 2001 AUMF repeal is harder because it requires a replacement framework — geographic limits, target organizations, sunset provisions, robust reporting. Substantive proposals exist (the Jayapal-Massie H.R. 6751 sunset bill introduced December 2025; Kaine-Young replacement framework variants); the legislative bandwidth and executive-branch resistance have constrained advancement.

Letters to your senators and representative are the operative lever. Senators on the Foreign Relations Committee and Armed Services Committee carry particular weight. Representatives on Foreign Affairs and Armed Services similarly. The substantive case for repeal is bipartisan; constituent pressure helps overcome legislative-bandwidth and institutional-resistance constraints.

Naming any specific operation that your representative has been asked to vote on (Yemen-related War Powers Resolutions; specific NDAA provisions; supplementary defense bills) gives the letter additional concreteness.
