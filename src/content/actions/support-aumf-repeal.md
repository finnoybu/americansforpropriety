Congress has not declared war since 1942. Subsequent military actions have proceeded under Authorizations for Use of Military Force (AUMFs), several of which have been used by multiple administrations far beyond their original scope.

The 2001 AUMF, passed three days after 9/11, has been used by four administrations to justify military operations in over a dozen countries — including operations against organizations that did not exist when the AUMF was passed. The 2002 Iraq AUMF and the 1991 Gulf War authorization remain technically in force despite the conflicts they authorized having ended decades ago.

Repeal of these stale authorizations has produced bipartisan majorities in both chambers in recent sessions. Senators Tim Kaine and Todd Young have led the effort across multiple Congresses. The 2002 AUMF repeal advanced through the Senate in 2023.

The 2001 AUMF repeal is harder because it requires a replacement framework — geographic limits, target organizations, sunset provisions, robust reporting. Substantive proposals exist (Kaine-Young replacement framework variants); the legislative bandwidth and executive-branch resistance have constrained advancement.

Letters to your senators and representative are the operative lever. Senators on the Foreign Relations Committee and Armed Services Committee carry particular weight. Representatives on Foreign Affairs and Armed Services similarly. The substantive case for repeal is bipartisan; constituent pressure helps overcome legislative-bandwidth and institutional-resistance constraints.

Naming any specific operation that your representative has been asked to vote on (Yemen-related War Powers Resolutions; specific NDAA provisions; supplementary defense bills) gives the letter additional concreteness.
