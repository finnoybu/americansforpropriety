US tenant law is unusually weak by comparison with most peer countries. Most US tenants have no just-cause protection — landlords can decline to renew leases for any reason or no reason. Most US tenants face eviction proceedings without legal representation while landlords have counsel.

The two most consequential tenant-protection reforms address these directly:

**Just-cause eviction** requires landlords to identify a specific cause for non-renewal (nonpayment, lease violation, owner move-in, etc.) rather than declining to renew at will. It shifts power back toward tenants without supply-side concerns rent control raises. Several states (NJ, CA, OR, WA, others) have enacted just-cause statewide; many cities have enacted local just-cause ordinances.

**Right to counsel in eviction proceedings** provides public legal representation for tenants. New York City, San Francisco, Cleveland, Newark, Philadelphia, and a growing list of cities have implemented some form of right to counsel. The empirical record: representation measurably reduces eviction rates and produces better outcomes for tenants, especially those with valid defenses or counterclaims that go undeveloped without legal support.

Both fights happen at the state and local level. State legislators, city council members, and county supervisors are the targets. Letters that name specific cases (your own or someone you know whose stability would have been preserved by either protection), local market conditions (where rents are rising and stability is at risk), or specific bills under consideration are most effective.

If you're a renter, your perspective carries particular weight. If you're a landlord who supports these reforms (some do; the political coalition is broader than often assumed), your perspective is also useful.
