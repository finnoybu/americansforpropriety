The 2022 Inflation Reduction Act introduced the corporate alternative minimum tax (CAMT) — a 15% floor on the largest corporations' effective tax rate based on book income. It is collecting meaningful revenue from a small number of very large firms whose regular tax rates had been historically low.

Several proposals would expand the CAMT: lower the threshold from $1B to $500M in book income, raise the rate to 18-21% for the largest firms, tighten definitions to capture more book-tax gaming, or extend it to better cover foreign subsidiary income.

The political fight over the CAMT is mostly invisible. It happens in Treasury rulemakings, JCT analyses, and quiet committee work. Constituent letters that make the case for expansion — or against weakening — punch above their weight in this kind of policy fight.

If you have a US Senator on the Finance Committee, or a House member on Ways and Means, your letter has particular weight. Members of those committees write the tax code.
