Independent redistricting commissions have a real, measurable track record now: more competitive districts, better community-of-interest preservation, higher public confidence in the process. California, Michigan, Arizona, Colorado, and several other states have established them in some form.

The 2030 census is the next major redistricting cycle. States that adopt commissions before then will have them in place; states that don't will draw partisan-controlled maps for another decade.

Active commission-adoption campaigns are underway in Ohio, Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, Florida, and others. The fights take different forms — constitutional amendment, ballot measure, statutory reform — but share a common political pattern: a partisan majority opposes commission adoption because it would reduce the majority's mapping advantage; the case for commissions has to be made directly to voters and to legislators in competitive districts.

If you live in a state without an independent commission, a letter to your state legislators is the most direct lever. State legislators read constituent mail more carefully than most federal offices. A specific, locally-grounded letter — naming a recent gerrymandering example or identifying a specific bill or amendment — has particular weight.
