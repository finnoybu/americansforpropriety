The Protecting the Right to Organize Act has passed the US House twice and stalled in the Senate twice. It has been reintroduced. The political math has not fundamentally changed, but the political attention on it has — driven by the most active labor-organizing wave in decades.

The PRO Act would: end captive-audience meetings, restore card-check union recognition, make first-contract arbitration the default when employers stall, override state right-to-work laws in the private sector, end permanent replacement of striking workers, apply the strict ABC test to misclassification, and impose meaningful civil penalties for unfair labor practices.

Senate offices count constituent contact. The threshold for an issue to "register" with senate staff is shockingly low — a few dozen calls or letters in a week from a state's residents. The work-product, when you write a personal letter, is read.

If your senator is undecided or formally on record as opposed, your letter is more valuable than if they're already a co-sponsor. (Co-sponsors should be thanked, not pressured.) Make it specific. Reference your own work or a workplace you know.
