The Post-9/11 GI Bill is one of the most generous federal education benefits programs in American history. It has also been a magnet for predatory practices by certain for-profit colleges that systematically targeted veterans because of a structural quirk in federal regulation (the 90/10 rule loophole that excluded GI Bill payments from the federal-aid count).

The 2021 reform addressed the 90/10 loophole. Two-plus years into implementation, the reform is producing measurable results. But several follow-on protections need ongoing defense:

**90/10 enforcement intensity.** Department of Education compliance with the new rule depends on staffing, priorities, and political environment. Continued constituent pressure for rigorous enforcement matters.

**Gainful employment rule durability.** The federal rule conditioning Title IV eligibility on whether a program's graduates can repay their loans has been adopted, repealed, and re-adopted multiple times depending on which administration controls the Department of Education. Statutory codification would prevent future cycles.

**Borrower-defense protection.** Veterans who attended fraudulent for-profits — many of which have since closed — are eligible for borrower-defense relief. Processing rates depend on Department of Education priorities; sustained pressure for processing matters.

**Veteran-specific protections.** The Veterans Education Empowerment Act and similar proposals strengthen veteran-specific protections in federal education regulation.

Letters to senators and representatives — particularly those on Veterans Affairs and Education and Workforce committees — push these reforms up the legislative agenda.

If you're a veteran who attended a for-profit college, or who knows someone who did, your perspective has weight. If you've been affected by a closed-school discharge, borrower-defense application, or 90/10 violation, naming that experience makes the policy concrete.
