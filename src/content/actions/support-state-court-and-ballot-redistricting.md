The 2019 Supreme Court ruling in Rucho v. Common Cause closed federal courts to partisan-gerrymandering claims under the Constitution. State-level work has filled the gap — state-court litigation under state constitutions, independent redistricting commissions adopted via ballot measure, and federal legislation that does not depend on federal-constitutional adjudication.

Several active fronts in 2025-2026:

**State supreme court elections.** State court composition determines whether state-constitutional theories of partisan gerrymandering are available in your state. Court elections matter for redistricting outcomes the way they matter for many other state-policy questions.

**Independent redistricting commission ballot measures.** Active in Ohio (multiple ballot attempts), Wisconsin (constitutional amendment process), and others. State-by-state, voters have repeatedly approved independent commissions when given the choice — the legislative path is harder because legislatures rarely give up redistricting authority voluntarily.

**Pre-2030-census state legislation.** The next redistricting cycle is approaching. States that adopt structural reforms (commissions, criteria, transparency requirements) before then will have them in place; states that don't will draw partisan-controlled maps for another decade.

**Federal Freedom to Vote Act and John Lewis Act.** Federal legislation that would establish national redistricting standards and restore VRA preclearance. Constituent pressure on senators is the operative lever.

If you live in a state with active redistricting reform — Ohio, Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, Florida, others — your state legislators (and your votes for state supreme court justices in states where they're elected) are the targets. Letters that name specific recent gerrymandering examples in your state, particular bills under consideration, and the timeline-to-2030 stakes carry weight.

If you live in a state with independent commissions already, supporting their continued integrity matters too — commissions face periodic political pressure to weaken their independence, and constituent support for the existing structure helps defend it.
