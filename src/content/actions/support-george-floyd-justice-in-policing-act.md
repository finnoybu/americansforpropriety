Qualified immunity is a Supreme Court-created doctrine that shields government officials from civil rights lawsuits unless they violated "clearly established law." In practice, the "clearly established" standard requires near-identical prior cases — and most cases die at the motion-to-dismiss stage because no prior case is sufficiently on point, even when the conduct is plainly unconstitutional.

The doctrine is not in the Constitution; it is not in any statute; it is the product of judicial decisions Congress can override. Several reform tracks:

**Federal abolition.** The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act and the Ending Qualified Immunity Act would amend Section 1983 to eliminate qualified immunity in federal civil rights claims. Has passed the House in prior sessions; stalled in Senate.

**Federal use-of-force standards.** Conditioning federal grants on adoption of specific use-of-force standards. Mandatory federal data reporting on stops and use of force.

**Demilitarization.** Restrictions on federal grant programs that channel military equipment to local police.

**State-level reforms.** Several states (NM, CO, others) have limited qualified immunity in state-law civil rights claims; expansion to additional states is an ongoing reform track.

Letters to senators and representatives on Judiciary committees move this up the legislative agenda. The substantive case is well-established; the political coalition for reform has not yet been sufficient to clear Senate procedural hurdles, but constituent pressure builds the case for future advancement.

If you've been involved in or affected by an incident where qualified immunity affected outcomes, your perspective has weight. If you're a current or former federal civil rights litigator, prosecutor, or policy advocate, your perspective is similarly valuable.
