State zoning preemption is the most consequential housing-policy development in a generation. Oregon, California, Washington, Massachusetts, Montana, Vermont, and a growing list of states have enacted laws that override exclusionary local zoning to allow multifamily and small-multi-unit construction in residential areas where it had been prohibited.

The early empirical record is encouraging. Permit data is up where reforms have taken effect. Multifamily projects are advancing in jurisdictions where they would have been illegal three years ago.

Active campaigns in 2025 include Connecticut, New York, New Jersey, Colorado, Maryland, Illinois, Minnesota, and others. Each fight is state-specific in design, but the structural pattern is consistent: state legislatures considering whether to preempt exclusionary local zoning to enable housing-supply growth.

If you live in a state where zoning reform is being debated, your state legislators are the targets. Letters that name specific local effects — a project that would not have been allowed under prior zoning, a price differential that supply expansion would address, a community-of-interest argument — carry weight. If you're a renter, a young person who can't afford to buy where you grew up, an aspiring homebuyer locked out of your local market, your perspective is particularly useful.

Note that zoning reform is most effective when paired with tenant protections. Letters supporting reforms that include both — supply expansion AND just-cause eviction, source-of-income protections, and similar — are well-positioned to push the next generation of legislation.
