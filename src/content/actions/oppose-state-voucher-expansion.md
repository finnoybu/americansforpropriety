Voucher and ESA programs have expanded rapidly in roughly 15 states since 2020. Universal ESAs in Arizona, Florida, Iowa, Indiana, West Virginia, and others now divert hundreds of millions to over a billion dollars annually from public school systems to private (often religious) schools — with most enrollment growth coming from families that already had children in private schools.

Where voucher expansions have been put to voters directly, they have lost — Kentucky 2024, Nebraska 2024 (twice), Colorado 2024. Where they advance through legislatures, the political pressure has been donor-driven and well-funded.

If your state is considering voucher or ESA expansion, your state legislators are the targets. Letters that connect the proposal to specific local effects (your district's per-pupil funding, a specific public school facing pressure, the empirical record on student outcomes) carry more weight than abstract policy arguments.

If you're a parent of a public-school student, a teacher, or a former public-school student, naming that in your letter is useful. If your state has had state-level adequacy litigation under your state constitution's education clauses, referencing it is also useful.
